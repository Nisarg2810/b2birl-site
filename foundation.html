/foundation/why

Small rooms first. Big stage last.

Most "marketing events" start with the ballroom and hope the people follow. We start with the people and build the ballroom around them.

Senior B2B marketers in Canada don't have a room of their own. The big gatherings happen south of the border, and the local ones tend to be vendor showcases or after-work mixers where nobody senior stays past the first drink. The gap isn't another conference. It's trust.

So B2B IRL runs as a media brand that happens to host events, not an events company that happens to post content. Every format below does two jobs: it gives the people in the room something real, and it produces the stories, opinions and data that make B2B IRL, and the person hosting it, the voice of B2B marketing in Canada.

Step 1 · Trust

Dinner Series

One long table, 15 to 20 senior B2B marketers, no stage. The most important event we run and the smallest.

Size15–20One table, never two
CadenceMonthlyToronto first, then rotating cities
Price to guestFreeStrictly invite-only
Funded by1 hostCovers venue & food

What it is

A host-led dinner. A restaurant, a brand or a member hosts; B2B IRL curates the guest list. Each dinner has exactly one host sponsor, who covers the venue and food in exchange for a seat at the table and a mention. No slides, no badges, no pitch.

Every dinner runs under the Chatham House rule: what's said can be used, but never attributed. That's what makes senior people talk.

Who it's for

  • CMOs and VPs of Marketing at B2B companies, roughly Series A through public.
  • Founders who still run marketing at companies past ~$5M ARR, by exception.
  • A mix every time: roughly a third SaaS, a third services/industrial B2B, a third wildcard.

Who it's not for

  • Directors and managers. Their room is the City Chapter.
  • Salespeople, recruiters and agency owners, however senior.
  • Anyone who asks "can I bring a colleague?" The seat is personal.

Run of the night

  1. 18:30 · ArrivalsOne drink, name cards already on the table. No name tags.
  2. 19:00 · SeatedHost welcome, 2 minutes max. Ross sets the table with one question.
  3. 19:15 · The questionEveryone answers it in turn over the first course, e.g. "What did you stop doing this year?"
  4. 20:00 · Open tableMains. The conversation goes wherever it goes. Seats swap after mains.
  5. 21:30 · CloseNo hard stop, but the bill is settled and nobody is sold anything.
  6. +48 hrsDouble opt-in intros, a thank-you note, and a recap piece (unattributed).

Who hosts (sponsors) it

  • Best fit VC and growth-equity firms. They want their portfolio CMOs in rooms like this and they never pitch at the table.
  • Good Martech, data and AI tools that sell to CMOs, if their own CMO or CEO takes the seat (not a sales rep).
  • Good Restaurants and hotels hosting in-kind, in exchange for a room full of people who book corporate dinners.
  • Good A member who wants to host at their own office or favourite spot.
  • No Agencies, consultancies, recruiters, lead-gen vendors. See why.

What the host gets, and doesn't

  • One seat at the table for a senior person. One.
  • A two-minute welcome, and "Hosted by" on the invite, menu card and recap.
  • A mention in the newsletter recap and Ross's post about the dinner.
  • A professional photo set from the night.
  • Not the guest list, emails or a follow-up sequence. Intros only if both sides opt in.

How we'll know it works

  • Show rate above 85% of confirmed guests.
  • At least 70% of the table is CMO or VP level.
  • Guests asking to come back, or nominating a peer.
  • A founding list of 50+ senior marketers by month three.
Ross's seat

Ross hosts every dinner in year one. He asks the opening question and listens more than he talks.

Afterwards he publishes "What 18 CMOs told me this month": the patterns, never the names. Over a year that can become the most-read B2B marketing column in Canada, written from rooms nobody else is in.

Step 2 · Engine

City Chapter

A half or full day in one city: CMO firesides, a short panel and structured networking for about 100 people.

Size~100Curated, not capped by the venue
CadenceQuarterlyPer city, run by a chapter lead
Price to guest$0 or $75–100Fee kills no-shows
Funded by2–4 sponsorsTitle down to community

What it is

The repeatable engine. Every quarter, in every live city, the same shape: two or three fireside chats with CMOs who've actually run the thing, one short panel, and networking that's designed rather than left to chance.

It's run under a chapter-lead model: a respected local marketer runs the city using the B2B IRL playbook. B2B IRL owns the brand, the sponsor relationships, the content and the standards.

Pricing: our recommendation

Two options are on the table: free with approval, or a small fee. We recommend a hybrid: $75–100 for general admission to keep no-shows under 15%, with senior invitees (Director+ from the dinner list), speakers and chapter members comped. Free events often lose 40–50% of registrants on the day; even a small fee cuts that sharply.

Who it's for

  • Mid-to-senior B2B marketers: managers, directors, heads of.
  • Founders who still own marketing.
  • The vendors who sell to them, capped at ~10% of the room, clearly badged.
  • A curated senior guest list (roughly 20 seats) keeps the room strong.

Run of the day (half-day version)

  1. 13:00 · DoorsCoffee, badges with a "working on" line instead of just a title.
  2. 13:30 · Ross opens10 minutes: "The state of B2B marketing in Canada, this quarter."
  3. 13:45 · Fireside 1A CMO, one campaign, the real numbers. 25 min + Q&A.
  4. 14:30 · Structured networkingTables by problem (pipeline, brand, AI, team), 3 rounds of 12 minutes.
  5. 15:15 · Fireside 2Title sponsor intro: 5 minutes, before this session only.
  6. 16:00 · PanelThree operators, one disagreement, 30 minutes.
  7. 16:45 · DrinksSupporting sponsor hosts. Ends 18:30.

The chapter lead

  • Who: a respected senior marketer in that city, not an event planner.
  • Does: local speakers, venue, guest curation, host on the day.
  • Gets: the title, a stage every quarter, an honorarium or 10–15% of chapter net, and a Homecoming seat.
  • Playbook covers: run-of-show, speaker brief, sponsor rules, guest criteria, content checklist, budget template.

Who sponsors it

  • Title Martech, data and AI platforms selling into marketing teams. Also banks, fintech and cloud providers with a B2B or startup practice.
  • Supporting The same categories, one tier down, plus VC firms who want dealflow and portfolio hiring.
  • Community Venues, coworking spaces, drinks brands, local associations, accelerators and media, in-kind or cross-promotion.
  • Community Recruiters and fractional-exec firms: community level only, never title.
  • No Agencies and consultancies at any tier. Lead-gen and list sellers. See why.

Step 3 · Skills

Marketing Workshops

Hands-on build sessions where marketers ship real work together. You leave with something built, not a notebook of quotes.

Size25–30Hard cap, for quality
CadenceEvery 1–2 monthsRotating topics
Price to guest$100–300Priced by the outcome
Funded byTickets0 sponsors, or 1 tool partner

What it is

A working marketer teaches. Not a vendor, not a guru: someone who did the thing last quarter. Laptops open, real accounts, real data where possible.

This is the one format that earns revenue without depending on a sponsor, and it gives the community value beyond networking.

Example sessions

  • ABM teardown ($150): bring your target account list and leave with a tiered program.
  • Outbound build ($200): a working sequence, copy and targeting, live by end of day.
  • AI workflows for marketing ops ($250): three automations built and running.
  • Positioning sprint ($300, full day): a messaging doc your CEO will sign off.
  • Content that drives pipeline ($150): a 90-day distribution plan for one hero piece.

Who it's for

  • Marketing managers and growth marketers who want skills.
  • In-house teams buying seats together, with a team rate for 3 or more.
  • Agency teams as attendees. Welcome to learn; not welcome to sponsor or teach-to-sell.
  • Anyone who grabs a seat. No application needed.

Sponsor model: almost none

  • Optional One tool partner, only if their tool is genuinely what the instructor would use anyway. They provide free credits or licences for attendees and can add CAD 1–2k.
  • The tool partner does not teach, pitch or get the attendee list. Their logo goes on the workbook, and that's it.
  • No Title sponsors, "presented by", or sponsor-led curriculum. The moment content is bought, the workshop is worthless.

Step 4 · Brand

Homecoming

A multi-day residency, ideally four days, for the boldest minds in B2B marketing. It launches in year 2, and not a day before it's earned.

Size300–400Application-based
CadenceYearlyFrom year 2
Price to guestPremiumTiered tickets
Funded byTitle + 5–8Full sponsorship package

What it is

Talks, workshops, dinners and a lot of deliberately unstructured time. Everything from steps one to three, in one place, for four days. Every chapter, every dinner regular, every workshop alumnus.

The launch gate: Homecoming only goes ahead once three or more chapters are running, each with at least two cycles, and at least half of chapter sponsors have renewed. Until then, it stays a promise.

Shape of the four days

  1. Day 1 · ArriveChapter-by-chapter welcome dinners. Ross's opening keynote at night.
  2. Day 2 · Main stageShort, sharp talks from operators. No vendor keynotes. Afternoon workshops.
  3. Day 3 · Tracks & dinnersDeep-dive tracks by role. Evening: 15 hosted dinners in the Dinner Series format.
  4. Day 4 · CloseThe state-of-the-room report, the Almanack launch, Ross's closing talk.

/foundation/rules

The sponsor rules.

Printed on every agreement. The room's trust is the product; sponsors rent proximity to it, never ownership.

  1. Stages are never for sale.Sponsors can suggest speakers. Editorial decides. A paid slot gets a 5-minute intro, clearly labelled.
  2. No attendee lists. Ever.Attendees can opt in to hear from a sponsor, per sponsor, per event. That's the only data that moves.
  3. Always labelled."Hosted by" and "Presented by" are shown plainly. No disguised content, no advertorial panels.
  4. Sponsor seats are capped.One seat at a dinner. No more than about 10% of any room is vendors, sponsors included.
  5. Category exclusivity at the top only.Titles and founding partners get it. Supporting and community tiers don't.
  6. Everyone gets a report.Within 7 days: attendance, seniority mix, opt-ins, content reach and photos. Renewals are earned, not assumed.
/foundation/never

Who we never take, and why.

Every "no" here protects the reason anyone senior shows up.

Agencies & consultancies

They sell services to exactly the people in the room. A sponsored agency turns a dinner into a pitch and makes the whole brand look like someone's funnel. Staying agency-free keeps B2B IRL neutral ground.

Lead-gen, list sellers & data brokers

Their business model is our attendees' contact details. Incompatible by definition.

Anyone who wants the list

If "we need the attendee list" is a condition of the deal, there is no deal, whatever the size of the cheque.

Pay-to-speak buyers

If it's conditional on a keynote, it's advertising dressed as content. The audience can tell, and they stop coming.

Recruiters at the dinner table

Welcome as community partners at chapters. At an intimate CMO dinner they make every guest feel like a target.

Other event organisers

Competing conferences want our audience list and our calendar. Cross-promotion case by case; sponsorship, no.

Reputation risks

Crypto, gambling, tobacco and vaping, and any company in an active public scandal. The brand is borrowed trust, and this spends it fast.

Sales-rep-only hosts

If a sponsor's seat at the table would be an account executive, not a senior leader, the answer is a chapter sponsorship instead.

/foundation/how

How to sponsor, step by step.

From first email to post-event report, in about four to six weeks for a chapter and two for a dinner.

  1. Say helloEmail hello@b2birl.com with "Sponsor" in the subject: who you are, who you sell to, which format interests you.
  2. Fit callA 20-minute call. We check category, conflicts and the rules above. About half of enquiries get redirected to a better-fit format.
  3. Pick the formatDinner, chapter tier, workshop tool partner or a bundle. Dates are locked on the calendar.
  4. One-page agreementDeliverables, rules and price on a single page. 50% on signing, 50% net-30 after the event.
  5. Co-planYour intro, your guest nominations (we have final say), your opt-in offer, logistics.
  6. Report & renewReport within 7 days. A renewal conversation within 30. Renewing sponsors get first pick of dates.

Prospective sponsors can also nominate a guest for a dinner. Nominations are reviewed like any other; a nomination is not a seat.

Why it works for Ross

Thought leadership isn't about having the loudest opinions. It's about having access nobody else has and turning it into a point of view. The dinners give Ross the access, the chapters give him a stage in every city, and the content gives him the reach.

A year in, he's the person who knows what Canada's B2B marketers are actually thinking, because he's the one who put them in the room.

Where Foundation fits: quietly

B2B IRL stands on its own name. Foundation is not a sponsor on stage, not in the run-of-show, and never pitched at a table. A single line, "B2B IRL is supported by Foundation", lives in the footer, and that's it.

That's deliberate. The moment the room smells like a funnel, the senior people leave. The halo lands on Ross because the room is clean, and it reaches the company from there.

Big mixers.
Marketing parties.
Not yet.

Why we skip them for now

  • They attract the wrong room. Open bars pull in vendors and job-seekers; senior marketers leave early and don't come back.
  • They're expensive and forgettable. The same budget runs four dinners that people talk about for a year.
  • They need a brand to carry them. A party works when the name on the invite is the draw. That comes after the media brand is strong, not before.
  • They dilute sponsors. A logo at a loud party is worth less than a seat at a quiet table.

When the time comes, the first one is the Homecoming after-party, and it's invite-only.
